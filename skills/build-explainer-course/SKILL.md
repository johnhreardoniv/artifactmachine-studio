# Build an explainer course

Create a page or course that helps a specified learner do something new. Match the user's requested format and audience. A short explainer may need no quiz; a multi-lesson course usually benefits from practice and feedback.

## Shape the learning journey

1. Establish the learner's starting knowledge, actual equipment or context, intended outcome, and constraints from the conversation and available project materials. Do not infer an exact product variant from a product family name.
2. Name the capability gained at the end. For a sequence, give each course a distinct job and let its output feed the next course. Avoid repeated beginner material under a new title.
3. For each lesson, choose one decision or skill, explain it in plain language, demonstrate it, then ask the learner to try it. The action should produce observable evidence where practical.
4. Mark examples, predictions, and untested steps as such. Define what would prove success. A status indicator or model output is not necessarily proof of the intended outcome.

## Research and teach

- Verify technical and time-sensitive details in current primary documentation. Use manufacturer instructions for hardware controls and the project's documentation for protocol or software behavior. Cite the page near the claim.
- Separate confirmed model facts from conditional advice. When a model is unknown, give a safe identification step and branch only on an actual label.
- Make the text sufficient on its own. Add a diagram, image, demo, or video only when it helps explain a mechanism or physical step. Link or credit its creator and license as required. Optional video must not hide an essential instruction.
- Choose examples from the learner's situation. Prefer a small real experiment to an abstract feature tour.

## Exercises and checks

- Write short quizzes that test the lesson's practical decision or a likely misconception. Use plausible alternatives, clear correct answers, and feedback that lets the learner revise. Do not make every question a vocabulary recall test.
- Give a course a capstone artifact that a learner can inspect: a tested route, configuration, plan, annotated example, or working build. Name the evidence and its limits.
- For multi-course journeys, state prerequisites and link the next course from the end of the current one.

## Build and verify

- Follow the existing project's design, accessibility, and publishing conventions. Make navigation, lesson state, quiz feedback, and progress understandable with keyboard and screen reader use.
- Check desktop and narrow layouts, all internal links, source links, images and credits, quiz scoring, and saved progress. Confirm the core lesson still works if external media is unavailable.
- After publication, load the live URL and confirm the intended pages and assets, not just a successful push.

Keep a short project-specific note about what worked, what was inferred, what remains untested, and what should change in the next iteration. Do not turn one topic's quirks into universal skill rules.
