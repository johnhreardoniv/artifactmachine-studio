LESSON 01 / BASELINE

Prepare one fair test.

A range number means little unless you can say what was tested, where, and with which settings.

Give each radio a job

For this course, call the fixed solar P1 Pro Solar, the portable SenseCAP Sense, and the Wio Wio. These are names for your experiment, not guesses about the trackers’ exact models. Begin with all three on a table, antennas attached, charged, and running Meshtastic. Record each exact model and firmware version from its label and app.

Match region, modem preset, frequency slot, and the channel you will use. Leave the three devices in CLIENT role. A client can already relay when useful; changing roles now would add a second variable. Verify that each phone or computer is connected to the intended node. A single client moving between nodes can show configuration, but two connected clients make a real send-and-receive test easier to prove.

Make a field log

Use paper or a spreadsheet. Each row should capture date and time, sender, receiver, location, placement, approximate distance, settings, whether the message arrived, and any hop or signal information the app exposes. Do not put private channel keys or a sensitive home location in a log you plan to share.

LESSON 02 / THREE-NODE ROUTE

Prove who heard whom.

A visible node and a delivered message answer different questions. Test the destination, then investigate the route.

First, prove delivery

Connect one client to Sense and another to Wio. Send a uniquely numbered short message, such as “test 01,” then have the recipient reply “test 01 received.” Repeat with Solar as one endpoint. Seeing the destination’s reply is stronger evidence than seeing a broadcast relay acknowledgment. Meshtastic’s routing behavior differs between broadcasts and direct messages, so write down which type you sent.

Next, put Solar where it may hear both portable nodes. Move Sense and Wio far enough apart or behind terrain so their direct path is weak. Send a new numbered message. The intended comparison is Solar available versus Solar absent or temporarily powered down, with the other settings and positions held steady. A result can vary from one attempt to another, so repeat it.

Use route tools carefully

The Traceroute module can report a path in both directions and per-hop SNR on supported firmware. It reports the first request packet to reach the destination; duplicate routes can exist, and nodes that cannot decrypt the channel may not appear. Treat a trace as a snapshot, then repeat it. A hop count alone does not name the relay.

Three-node relay exercise

Log three numbered messages with Solar on, and three in the same positions with Solar off. Mark delivery at the receiving client, not just at the sender. If the results are mixed, record that uncertainty rather than claiming a proven relay.

LESSON 03 / COVERAGE

Draw a map from evidence.

Useful coverage is a set of places where a message arrives reliably, not the farthest single packet you have ever heard.

Choose a repeatable route

Keep Solar fixed. Walk the portable node through several safe, accessible stops: a nearby street, a low spot, behind a building, and an open vantage point. At each stop send the same small set of numbered messages in both directions. Log arrivals and failures, approximate position, obstructions, and any displayed SNR or hops. A sample such as “4 of 5 received” says more than one lucky packet.

Meshtastic’s Range Test module can send sequential packets from a fixed node and save received locations as CSV on the mobile side. Enable it only for the test, with the necessary location settings deliberately chosen. It consumes airtime and should be turned off afterward. A manual message log remains useful when a tracker lacks the location capability or you prefer not to record precise coordinates.

Compare prediction with observation

Open the Meshtastic Site Planner and enter the approximate solar site, antenna height, band, and receiver assumptions. Its terrain-based map is a prediction. Mark real pass, mixed, and fail stops on a separate map. Look for differences caused by buildings, trees, indoor placement, or model assumptions; do not silently adjust the prediction to fit your result.

Coverage artifact

Make a three-color map: reliable, mixed, and untested or failed. Add the date, settings, route, sample count, and a note that conditions may change.

LESSON 04 / PLACEMENT & POWER

Improve the weak spots.

Move the radio path before changing the protocol. Then make sure the improved site can stay powered.

Test a placement hypothesis

Choose one mixed or failed stop from your map. Form a concrete hypothesis: “Solar is behind the roofline,” or “the antenna is blocked by metal.” Move Solar to a safe, clearer temporary position or vary its height while keeping the portable stops and settings the same. Repeat the numbered messages. Compare the before and after counts; note any new dead spots. Do not infer that more height always helps every direction.

For a permanent installation, follow Seeed’s P1 Pro mounting instructions: secure the bracket, protect the cable and enclosure, and tilt the panel so water does not collect. Keep the supplied LoRa antenna attached before powering. Recheck public position settings before placing a GPS-equipped node at a private site.

Watch power over days

A sunny afternoon is not a solar-power test. Meshtastic device telemetry includes battery level, voltage, channel utilization, and airtime. Record battery at roughly the same time each day across a sunny and cloudy interval. Seeed’s P1 Pro documentation lists charging limits, so expect temperature and shade to affect the trend. If battery level falls day after day, review exposure and traffic before trusting the location unattended.

Before-and-after trial

Choose one placement change and one weak stop. Log delivery counts before and after, plus the solar node’s battery level for several days. Keep a photograph or sketch of both placements.

LESSON 05 / FIELD RECOVERY

Diagnose without guessing.

A failed message can be a phone, node, configuration, power, or radio-path problem. Check from the nearest layer outward.

Follow the path

  1. Client to node: Does the app still connect to the intended radio? If not, check that node’s charge, power control, Bluetooth permissions, and model-specific guide.
  2. Node to node nearby: Bring the radios close. Compare region, modem preset, frequency slot, channel name and key. Confirm they run Meshtastic.
  3. Destination delivery: Send a new numbered message and get a reply from the actual recipient. Check whether a channel broadcast or direct message was used.
  4. Field path: Return to the last known working stop, then move through the weak area. Check placement, antenna attachment, obstacles, and battery trend.

Record each check and its result. “The app connected, nearby messages arrived, but the route failed behind the hill” points to a different fix from “the app cannot find the tracker.” Avoid broad resets before saving current settings and identifying the exact hardware variant.

Define your working route

A course capstone is a practical route card: where Solar sits, two tested endpoint areas, required settings, a map of repeatable results, a battery observation, and one fallback if the route fails. Include the date and limits. It is evidence for a supplementary communication path, not a promise that every message will arrive.

Capstone · Route card
  • Map with tested pass, mixed, and fail areas
  • At least one verified two-way exchange between portable nodes
  • Solar-on versus Solar-off comparison or a labeled “not proven” result
  • One placement comparison and a multi-day battery trend
  • A short failure procedure another person could follow
